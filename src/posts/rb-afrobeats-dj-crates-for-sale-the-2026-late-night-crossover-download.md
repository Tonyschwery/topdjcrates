## Building the Bridge: Where R&B Meets Afrobeats on the BPM Chart

Most contemporary R&B sits in the 85-100 BPM range, often half-time against a 170-200 BPM feel. Afrobeats and Afrobeats-adjacent records typically run 100-112 BPM with a swung, syncopated pocket. That overlap zone — **100 to 108 BPM** — is your bridge. It's where a half-time R&B groove and an Afrobeats mid-tempo record can sit on the same Camelot wheel without a jarring tempo jump.

Practical approach:

1. **Sort your R&B folder by BPM, not just by artist or year.** Pull anything landing 95-105 BPM into a dedicated transition crate.
2. **Match energy before you match key.** A moody, minor-key R&B cut bridges cleaner into a melodic Afrobeats record than a hard rap banger does.
3. **Use the vocal as your glue.** If the outgoing R&B track has a strong hook, let it ride over the incoming Afrobeats intro before you commit to the full switch.
4. **Keep two acapella outs on standby** — one R&B, one Afrobeats — so you can lay a familiar vocal over a new rhythm bed and buy yourself four extra bars to read the crowd.
5. **Always have an instant retreat option.** If the floor doesn't move with the new groove, you need a known R&B banger cued and ready within two bars.

## How Do You Program the Transition Without Losing the Floor?

Don't think of this as "switching genres." Think of it as one continuous arc with four stages:

- **Stage one — the hook:** Slow-to-mid R&B, strong vocal presence, minimal mixing complexity. Let people sing.
- **Stage two — the lean:** Introduce 100-105 BPM Afrobeats-adjacent R&B collabs. This is where the crossover singles from Dave, Tems, and similar artists live — the crowd already knows these records from both worlds.
- **Stage three — the commit:** Full Afrobeats, 104-110 BPM, percussion up front, vocal hooks still carrying the emotional thread from stage one.
- **Stage four — the lift:** Amapiano or log-drum peak-time, built on the groundwork you laid without anyone noticing the genre actually changed three times.
