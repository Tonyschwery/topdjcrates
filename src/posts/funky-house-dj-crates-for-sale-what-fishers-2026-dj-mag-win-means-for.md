## What genre is FISHER actually playing?

Technically it sits between funky house and tech house — some call it "bounce house," some just call it FISHER-core. The ingredients are consistent across his sets and the artists orbiting him (Chris Lake, Dom Dolla, John Summit, Mau P):

- **124-127 BPM**, rarely higher
- Filtered disco or funk samples looped into the groove, not just dropped once
- A vocal hook that's almost chant-like — easy to sing back after two listens
- Drums mixed hot and dry, built for a system, not headphones
- Short, DJ-friendly intros so the track mixes in clean on the first bar

That last point matters more than people admit. If a track takes 90 seconds to get to the groove, it's not getting played on a real night. Everything in this lane is built to **drop straight into a mix**.

## How to build a bounce-house set that actually mixes

You don't need a dozen new tools to pull this off. You need the right files and a plan:

1. **Sort by BPM first, key second.** Keep everything in the 123-128 range in one Rekordbox or Serato crate so transitions stay smooth without pitch-bending a track out of its pocket.
2. **Group by Camelot key** within that BPM band. Funky house tracks often share compatible minor keys — harmonic mixing here lets you blend the bassline under a new vocal instead of cutting hard.
3. **Front-load short-intro tracks.** Use them as your mix-in points. Save the longer builds for moments when you actually want the floor to breathe.
4. **Keep acapella versions on hand.** A hook from one track layered over another's groove is the fastest way to make a FISHER-style mashup moment live, without touching a DAW mid-set.
5. **Test every file at club volume before the gig.** A funky bassline that sounds great in headphones can turn muddy on a big rig if the low end isn't mixed right — which is exactly why **studio-grade WAV quality** matters more in this genre than almost any other.
