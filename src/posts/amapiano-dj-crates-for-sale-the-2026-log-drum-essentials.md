## What BPM is amapiano?

Forget the guesswork. Working amapiano producers and DJs converge on a **108–118 BPM band**, with the genre center sitting around 112–113 BPM. Push toward 118–120 BPM if you're blending into Afro house or peak-time Afrobeats, and stay closer to 110–113 BPM if you're building a slower, log-drum-forward groove for a lounge or terrace crowd. Anything faster than 120 and you've crossed into Afro house territory — anything slower than 108 and you're basically playing deep house with a piano riff.

That tight range is actually good news for you. It means amapiano mixes almost as easily as tech house or Afro house, which is exactly why it's showing up in more open-format sets this year instead of staying siloed as its own late-night block.

## Which Camelot keys actually work for amapiano log drums?

Amapiano leans melodic — jazzy chords, soulful keys, vocal hooks — so **key-matching matters more here than in stripped-back tech house**. Catalog data on current amapiano tracks shows a cluster around Camelot 11A, 9A, and 4A, which tells you most of what you'll buy sits comfortably on the minor side of the wheel. Before you drop a new log drum track into a set:

1. **Tap or check the BPM first.** Don't trust the label — verify it against your own analysis in Rekordbox or Serato.
2. **Check the Camelot key**, not just the BPM. A perfect tempo match with a clashing key still sounds wrong once that piano riff comes in.
3. **Listen for the log drum pattern itself.** It should sit low and slide, not thump like a four-on-the-floor kick. If it doesn't slide, it's not really amapiano — it's house with a piano sample slapped on top.
4. **Confirm the intro has clean room to mix in.** A lot of amapiano exports come with vocal-forward intros; you want at least 16–32 bars of instrumental log drum before the hook lands.
5. **Tag it properly.** Genre, BPM, and key tags in your library save you ten seconds mid-set that you don't have.
