## What Is G-House, Really?

G-house — short for "gangsta house" — fuses four-on-the-floor house drums with chopped hip-hop vocals and a dark, swaggering bassline. Think West Coast rap aesthetics dropped over a house groove. The tempo range sits roughly between 118 and 128 BPM, with most working examples landing closer to 122-126 so they sit comfortably next to tech house and funky house in a set.

The telltale signs:

- **Syncopated, swung drum programming** instead of a straight tech house pulse
- **Low-slung, filtered basslines** that growl more than they stab
- **Vocal chops and rap samples** used as percussive hooks, not full verses
- A **dark, gritty tone** that reads as street-level rather than polished festival-main-stage

It's close cousin to jackin house and tech house, which is exactly why it mixes so easily into sets built around those genres.

## Why G-House Is Back in 2026

House music in general is having a moment — industry reporting this year has flagged Dance/Electronic as the fastest-growing genre category, with the sound pulling energy away from a single polished template and toward African, Latin, and UK-rooted percussion. G-house fits that same current: it's house music that doesn't sand off its rough edges.

Crowds that grew up on hip-hop recognize the vocal chops instantly. That recognition is the whole point — G-house gives you a bridge record between a hip-hop block and a house-driven back half of your set, without the awkward tempo jump that kills momentum on the floor.

## How to Mix G-House Into Your Set

You don't need a dedicated G-house-only hour to use this genre well. Treat it as a connective tissue track type:

1. **Bridge from hip-hop to house.** Drop a G-house cut right after an R&B/hip-hop block to carry the crowd's energy into four-on-the-floor territory without losing them.
2. **Match BPM before you match genre.** G-house's 118-128 range overlaps with funky house and tech house — use that overlap, not a hard genre switch, to keep transitions smooth.
3. **Lean on the vocal chop as your mix point.** Those chopped hooks are built to be scratched in and out — use them the same way you'd use an acapella out.
4. **Check Camelot keys before you blend basslines.** G-house basslines are loud in the mix; a key clash will be obvious fast.
5. **Keep your intro/outro edits clean.** G-house tracks often start mid-groove — a proper intro edit saves you from fumbling the mix live.
