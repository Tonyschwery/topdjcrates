## What flamenco house actually sounds like on a turntable

Strip away the branding and the structure is familiar to anyone who already spins Latin house or afro house:

- **Tempo**: mostly 120-124 BPM, same lane as deep house and Latin house
- **Percussion-forward arrangements**: palmas (handclaps), cajón, and castanets doing the job that shakers and congas do in afro house
- **Vocal tension**: flamenco cante vocals used sparingly, often pitched or filtered rather than sung straight through the drop
- **Groove over melody**: the hook is rhythmic, not a big synth lead — it rewards DJs who already know how to layer percussion tracks

That overlap with Latin house and afro house is the point. You don't need a separate genre folder. You need the right transitional tracks to bridge into it.

## How to actually program the transition

1. **Scout your key and BPM first.** Flamenco house cuts cluster around 120-124 BPM in Camelot keys built on minor/Phrygian flavors — check your Rekordbox or Serato key tags before you plan the bridge, not during the set.
2. **Open with the familiar.** Play a Latin house or afro house track the crowd already recognizes the groove of. Let them settle into the percussion pattern.
3. **Bridge on the drums, not the melody.** Use the percussion break or an intro edit to swap in the flamenco element — palmas and cajón sit in a different frequency pocket than a standard clap/shaker loop, so EQ out competing highs before you blend.
4. **Hold the vocal for the second half.** If the flamenco cante vocal is the hook, don't lead with it. Let the room lock into the rhythm first, then bring the vocal in on the second phrase for the payoff.
5. **Exit the same way you entered.** Mix back out through another Latin or afro house cut. The genre tag changes; the dancefloor's muscle memory doesn't have to.
