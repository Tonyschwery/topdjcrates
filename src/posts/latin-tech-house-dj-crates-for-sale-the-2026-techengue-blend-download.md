## What is "techengue" and why is every DJ searching for it?

Open TikTok, YouTube, or Beatport right now and you'll see the same tag everywhere: **techengue**. It's the shorthand DJs and producers coined for the collision of reggaeton and Latin percussion with tech house's rolling low end. Latin American producers aren't just sprinkling congas on generic loops anymore — they're building full tech house records around reggaeton vocal chops, dembow snares, and timbale fills. Beatport and BPM Supreme both run dedicated Latin Tech House charts now, and Toolroom has a whole sample line built around the sound.

For working DJs, this isn't a niche curiosity. It's a **dance floor bridge**. It lets you move from a straight reggaeton set into a tech house peak-time block — or the other way around — without losing the crowd on the tempo change. If you've ever watched a floor go dead the second you jump from 95 BPM reggaeton into 128 BPM tech house, techengue is the fix.

## Why techengue works on a real dance floor

Reggaeton and tech house share more DNA than people think. Both genres are built on repetition, syncopation, and a vocal hook that rewards call-and-response crowd energy. What techengue does is slow tech house down slightly, or double-time reggaeton's dembow pattern, so the two sit in a shared pocket — usually somewhere between **122 and 128 BPM**. That's the same range your Camelot wheel already lives in for most tech house sets, which means you're not reinventing your mixing approach. You're just widening your crate.

This matters for three kinds of gigs:

- **Latin nights that need a peak-time lift** without abandoning the crowd's core sound
- **Open-format club sets** where you want a smooth ramp from Latin/urban into house
- **Wedding and private events** with mixed-heritage crowds who want both worlds in one set

## Mixing techengue into your set: a practical approach

You don't need a new workflow. You need a plan for where the bridge happens.

- **Sort by BPM first, key second.** Techengue tracks tend to cluster in the low-to-mid 120s. Build a Rekordbox or Virtual DJ playlist filtered to that range before the gig, so you're not hunting mid-set.
- **Use acapella outs from your reggaeton crate** to layer over tech house instrumentals during the transition. This is where studio-quality **acapella edits** earn their keep — a clean vocal stem lets you tease the Latin element on top of a house groove before you fully commit to the switch.
- **Watch your Camelot wheel.** Most techengue and Latin tech house records sit around 8A/8B to 10A/10B. Match energy, not just key, so the transition feels like a rise, not a genre swap.
- **Test the bridge in a low-stakes set first.** Try it in a bar set or a warm-up slot before you run it on a packed peak-time floor. You want to know exactly which two tracks make the handoff seamless.
