## What Is Dark Disco?

Dark disco takes the four-on-the-floor DNA of classic disco and nu-disco and strips out the brightness. Think 1980s-inspired retro synths, atmospheric pads, minor-key basslines, and the occasional electric guitar line cutting through the fog. It's still **danceable** — that's non-negotiable for the genre — but it trades disco's grin for a smirk. Artists in the space describe it as disco's "devious" side: groove-forward, hypnotic, built for rooms lit low.

It sits comfortably next to indie dance and dark disco crossovers that have been building steam all year, which is exactly why our catalogue groups them together.

## What BPM Is Dark Disco?

This is where a lot of DJs get tripped up. Dark disco doesn't have one fixed tempo — it loosely spans **80 to 130 BPM**, but the sweet spot most working DJs actually play sits closer to **105–120 BPM**. That overlaps cleanly with nu-disco and slower disco house, which makes it one of the easier "new" genres to blend into an existing set without a jarring tempo jump.

Practical takeaway: if your current disco house selections sit around 122–124 BPM, dark disco tracks in the 115–120 range give you a natural down-shift point in your set, not a stop-and-restart.

## Where Dark Disco Fits in Your Set

Dark disco isn't a peak-time weapon in the way a tech house anthem is. It's a **mood tool**. Use it to:

1. **Open a set** — moody, groove-heavy dark disco pulls a room in before you build energy, without giving away your best cards early.
2. **Bridge a transition** — moving from a chilled organic or downtempo segment into house? Dark disco is the missing middle gear.
3. **Close a night** — after peak time burns out, a dark disco run-out keeps people dancing instead of reaching for their coats.
4. **Reset the energy mid-set** — if the floor's been hammered with 128 BPM tech house for 40 minutes, a dark disco pocket gives ears (and feet) a breather without killing momentum.

## How to Mix Dark Disco Into a Working Set

Here's a practical approach that works in Rekordbox, Serato, or Virtual DJ:

- **Sort by Camelot key first, BPM second.** Dark disco's minor-key basslines make harmonic clashes obvious fast. A clean key match matters more here than in brighter, major-key disco.
- **Use the intro/outro edit, not the radio cut.** Dark disco tracks often have long, atmospheric intros in the original mix — a proper DJ edit trims the fat so you're not waiting 90 seconds to find the beat.
- **Ride the low end carefully.** Dark disco basslines are thicker and more sustained than classic disco. EQ out competing bass on the incoming track before you bring it up, or you'll get mud.
- **Watch for acapella outs.** Dark disco vocals are moody and repetitive — great for layering over a percussive dark disco or Afro house instrumental if your **crate** has clean acapella stems.
- **Don't rush the blend.** This genre rewards a slow, 16-to-32-bar mix over a quick cut. Let the groove overlap do the work.
