Reggaeton never left. But right now it's doing something that catches DJs off guard mid-set: **splitting into two distinct tempo lanes**. Classic-leaning reggaeton is sitting in the 85–100 BPM pocket, while the newer wave of dembow riddims is pushing 110–130 BPM. This is the real gap right now. Everyone's writing about Bad Bunny's chart dominance. Almost nobody is writing about the actual mixing problem: how do you move a crowd from a slow-grind perreo record into a fast dembow riddim without the energy collapsing? That's what this post is for.

## What BPM is reggaeton, actually?

Reggaeton has always lived around **85–100 BPM**, built on the dembow riddim's boom-ch-boom-chick pattern. That's your foundation tempo — the one most casual listeners associate with the genre.

But "dembow" as its own lane, especially the Dominican-flavored strain blowing up in 2026, runs hotter: **110–130 BPM**. Same rhythmic DNA, double-time feel. Mix these back to back without a plan and you'll either drag the fast tracks or rush the slow ones. Either way, the floor feels it before you do.

## Why this matters for your set right now

Working DJs are already reporting this in their weekly mixes — reggaeton, dembow, and house getting blended in the same hour of a party set. Wedding DJs are doing it too: 2026 reception playlists are jumping genre and era in the same 20-minute block, and nobody's flinching. That's the new normal. Genre-fluid sets are the expectation, not the exception.

Which means your reggaeton records can't just live in isolation anymore. They need to connect forward into dembow, and dembow needs to connect forward into house or Afrobeats without a tempo cliff.

## The Camelot key problem nobody talks about

Reggaeton and dembow productions lean almost entirely on **minor keys** — Aeolian and Dorian, mostly. In Camelot terms, that means the bulk of your crate clusters around **5A, 6A, 7A, and 8A**. This is actually good news: it means harmonic mixing inside the genre is easier than people assume, because so much of the catalog sits on adjacent wheel positions.

The catch is melodic reggaeton (the more pop-leaning, Balvin-style records) drifts further from that cluster than the stripped-down, percussion-forward dembow cuts. **Check the key column before you build a transition, not after.** A clash here is obvious to a dancing crowd even when they can't name what's wrong.

## How to mix the reggaeton-to-dembow tempo gap

Here's the actual sequence that works on a real floor:

1. **Group your crate by tempo lane first, genre second.** Tag everything 85–100 BPM as your "foundation" reggaeton set and everything 110–130 BPM as your "riddim" or dembow set. Don't rely on genre metadata alone — it lies constantly.
2. **Use a half-time or double-time bridge track.** Look for a dembow cut that reads at 115 BPM but has a strong half-time feel — it'll sit comfortably against a 95 BPM classic without a jarring pitch fader move.
3. **Ride the pitch fader in small increments across two or three tracks**, not one big jump. A 6-8% nudge per transition keeps the crowd's feet in sync the whole way up.
4. **Use an acapella or intro edit to mask the tempo climb.** A vocal intro at the new tempo gives dancers a second to adjust before the low end hits full force.
5. **Land on a Camelot-compatible key** for the first drop after the climb. This is where a pre-tagged, harmonically sorted crate saves you — you're not guessing under pressure.
6. **Keep a house or Afrobeats bridge ready** for when you need to exit the dembow lane entirely. 124-126 BPM house tracks share enough rhythmic footing with fast dembow to make that exit smooth.
